# ETS calibration: how ETS raters actually apply the Writing rubrics

Use this file for the **rubric score**, the number that predicts what ETS would give (in the toefl-coach system, the **ETS estimate**).

The older reading in `rubrics.md` §3–5 and `anchors.md` is deliberately stricter than ETS. In the toefl-coach system it is kept, unchanged, as the strict lens (**Strict estimate**). Do not use it to predict the ETS score.

**Sources:**
- ETS, *The Official Guide to the TOEFL iBT Test, Pocket Edition* (2026): Writing chapter, pp. 205–218; scoring notes, p. 36.
- ETS, *Updated TOEFL iBT Test Overview*: Writing scoring guides.

ETS material is not reproduced here; its features are summarized.

**How these rules were calibrated (blind, 2026-10-24):**
- A grader using the older strict reading gave ETS's rated-5 samples a 3 or a 4.
- These rules were then written from the calibration half of the samples and tested by a fresh blind grader on the held-out half. On the held-out half, Writing moved from 3 of 6 to 5 of 6 responses within ETS's level; with Speaking included, from 8 of 11 to 10 of 11. All held-out responses landed within ±1, and the low examples and controls stayed low.
- The one remaining miss (an Academic Discussion post that synthesizes both classmates) led to rule 4's synthesis clause. That clause was added after the held-out test and is not independently validated.
- Full report: toefl-coach `references/ets-calibration-report.md`.

## What ETS says about scoring (Guide)
- Responses are understood as **first drafts**, and "you can earn a high score with a response that contains some errors" (p. 36).
- **Email** (p. 207):
  - Responding to **all required points** is a very important criterion.
  - A concise message is fine if it is complete, relevant and clearly organized, but it must not just repeat the prompt.
  - Ideas need to be connected and easy to follow; paragraphs are not required.
  - Variety of language should be natural, and varied structures used for their own sake do not help.
  - Minor mistakes must be "typical of competent writers working under timed conditions".
- **Academic Discussion** (pp. 212–218):
  - Contributing to the discussion is a very important criterion. Agreeing with a classmate and elaborating in your own words counts; so do disagreeing and new ideas.
  - High-level responses do not need several arguments: one detailed, well-elaborated argument is enough for a 5.
  - Responses under 100 words "typically do not demonstrate" the development needed for a 5.
- **Hard limits** (pp. 207, 215–216):
  - Language that is hard to follow, overly simple sentences and limited vocabulary give "no higher than a 3, no matter how well" the content addresses the task.
  - Memorized or formulaic text (wordy openings and closings, canned examples or "polls") is not credited and scores "considerably lower".
  - Content-free wordiness gets "just 1 or 2", even at 100–200 words.

## Rules for the rubric score (ETS reading)
1. **Task first.**
   - **Email:** each required point must be addressed with at least one relevant detail. One or two sentences per point is enough.
   - **Academic Discussion:** a clear position supported by at least one reason or example. Agreeing with a classmate and elaborating counts, and so does rebutting one.
2. **Read charitably.** Ask whether the message comes across on first reading, not whether each argument is convincing or each sentence elegant. A weaker argument next to stronger ones does not pull a response down a level.
3. **"Consistent facility" is not "error-free" or "sophisticated".** It means:
   - some variety of structures (subordinate and relative clauses, conditionals, linking)
   - some natural idiomatic phrasing
   - errors that do not interfere with meaning

   ETS's rated-5 samples carry about 2–3 non-impeding genuine errors per 100 words (agreement, articles, comma splices, word form, an awkward structure) on top of typos. Plain everyday vocabulary is fine when it is appropriate.
4. **5 vs 4.** Give **5** when the task is fully done, the ideas are clear and connected, and the language shows consistent facility as defined in rule 3. Give **4** when one of these is noticeably weaker:
   - a required point is only mentioned, with no detail
   - the sentences are mostly simple, with little variety
   - errors are frequent enough that some sentences need rereading
   - a register or convention lapse (Email)
   - an Academic Discussion under 100 words, or one that stays at the level of assertions

   **Do not** use error counts, "precision" or "richness" of vocabulary as the 5/4 tiebreaker; ETS does not.

   **Synthesis counts as elaboration.** Reasons that build on the classmates' or the professor's points are real elaboration when two things hold:
   - they are explained in the writer's own words
   - they are combined into the writer's own position, even a balanced one ("use history as a guide, but…")

   A personal example is not required for a 5; ETS's rated-5 history post has none. A post stays at 4 only if it merely restates the posts without its own explanation or position.
5. **4 vs 3.**
   - **3:** a required point is missing or every point is thin; errors are noticeable throughout; or the range is narrow and simple. The meaning still comes across.
   - **4:** the reader understands everything on first reading, and the task is done with adequate detail.
6. **Caps (ETS):**
   - Email with a required point missing: usually no higher than 3.
   - Academic Discussion with no identifiable position: no higher than 3.
   - Language hard to follow, overly simple and limited: no higher than 3.
   - Academic Discussion under 100 words: rarely 5.
   - Mostly memorized or formulaic: 1–2.
   - Content-free wordiness: 1–2.
7. **Ties.** If still torn between two levels after rules 1–6, give the lower score and mark it borderline (*4, granica 5*). Decide by the rubric's general description ("fully successful" vs "generally successful"), not by counting errors.
