The official guides are holistic: one description per level. Below, each level is broken into dimensions so judgments stay consistent; the wording is a paraphrase, so check the PDF when exact wording matters. The final score is still holistic: the level whose overall picture fits best.

**Score 0, both tasks:** blank, rejects the topic, not in English, copied entirely from the prompt, entirely unrelated to the prompt, or arbitrary keystrokes.

## 1. Write an Email

| Dimension | 5 fully successful | 4 generally successful | 3 partially successful | 2 mostly unsuccessful | 1 unsuccessful |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Overall effect | Effective and clearly expressed, language control consistent throughout | Mostly effective, easy to understand, language adequate for the task | Task generally accomplished, but language limits keep parts of the message from being fully clear or effective | An attempt, but mostly ineffective; message limited or hard to interpret | Ineffective; message may be close to unintelligible |
| Elaboration | Details that clearly serve the email's purpose | Enough support for the purpose | Supports the purpose only in part | Limited or irrelevant | Very little, if any |
| Range | Varied syntax used effectively; precise, idiomatic word choice | Some syntactic variety; appropriate word choice | Moderate range of syntax and vocabulary | Some connected sentences, but a narrow range | Telegraphic: short or disconnected phrases, very limited vocabulary |
| Accuracy | Only the slips a competent writer makes under time pressure | Few errors | Some noticeable errors in structure, word forms or idiom | Errors accumulate | Serious and frequent |
| Social conventions | Consistently appropriate: politeness, register for the recipient, order of information, well-formed requests, refusals, complaints | Mostly appropriate | Some noticeable lapses | not described separately | not described separately |
| Own language | | | | | Little original language; coherent parts mostly lifted from the prompt |

What social conventions look like in practice: a greeting that fits the recipient (Dear Professor Kim / Dear Ms. Carter / Hi Sam); the purpose early; information in a sensible order, roughly one paragraph per bullet point; requests softened (Would it be possible to..., I was wondering whether...); complaints firm but courteous; a closing line and sign-off. Register can miss in either direction: texting style to a manager, or a stiff essay voice to a friend.

## 2. Write for an Academic Discussion

| Dimension | 5 fully successful | 4 generally successful | 3 partially successful | 2 mostly unsuccessful | 1 unsuccessful |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Overall effect | Relevant, very clearly expressed contribution; consistent language control | Relevant contribution; ideas easy to understand | Mostly relevant and mostly understandable; some language control | An attempt to contribute, but language limits make ideas hard to follow | Ineffective attempt; language limits keep ideas from coming through |
| Elaboration | Relevant explanations, examples or details, well elaborated | Relevant, adequately elaborated | Part of an explanation, example or detail is missing, unclear or irrelevant | Ideas poorly elaborated or only partly relevant | Words and phrases signal an attempt, but few or no coherent ideas |
| Range | Varied structures used effectively; precise, idiomatic word choice | A variety of structures; appropriate word choice | Some variety; a range of vocabulary | Limited range | Severely limited |
| Accuracy | Only competent-writer slips | Few errors | Some noticeable errors in sentence structure, word form or idiom | Errors accumulate | Serious and frequent |
| Own language | | | | | Little original language; coherent parts mostly lifted from the prompt |

What a contribution looks like in practice: the post answers the professor's question, takes a recognisable position and adds something, such as a new reason, an example, a distinction, or a qualified middle position, rather than restating the classmates. Engaging a classmate by name ("Unlike Tom, I think...") is natural and helps; agreeing and repeating is not a contribution. The register is a thoughtful class forum, not a five-paragraph essay. Memorised template lines ("This is a very controversial topic in today's society") use up time and words without adding elaboration.

## 3. Caps

Rules of thumb, not ETS rules. They stop one serious weakness from being averaged away.

- Email: a required bullet point not addressed: usually no higher than 3. Two missing: usually 2.
- Email: register clearly wrong for the recipient (texting style to a manager, a demanding or rude complaint): usually no higher than 3.
- WAD: no identifiable position, or nothing beyond the classmates' points: usually no higher than 3.
- WAD under 100 words: rarely has the elaboration a 4 needs. A strong signal, not an automatic cap.
- Either task: more than one or two sentences whose meaning can't be recovered: usually 2, at most 3.

## 4. Separating adjacent levels

**5 vs 4.** A 5 is precise, not merely correct: the right word and collocation, structures used for effect rather than just present, every point developed with specific detail. A 4 reads easily but has some plain or slightly unidiomatic phrasing, a few genuine errors, or elaboration that is adequate rather than rich. Quick test: three or more genuine (non-slip) errors in 120-150 words means it is not a 5.

**4 vs 3.** At 3 the reader notices problems: a point is thin, generic or unclear; errors make you pause (wrong word forms, tense or agreement, broken idioms) even though the meaning survives; sentences are mostly simple or compound, with the same few linkers (also, because, so). At 4 everything is easy to follow on first reading.

**3 vs 2.** At 2 the reader has to work: several sentences need rereading, ideas are hard to follow or barely developed, the range is narrow.

**2 vs 1.** At 1 there are few coherent ideas; the language is fragmentary or mostly copied.

**Error density, as a tiebreaker only** (genuine errors per 100 words, slips excluded): about 0-1 fits a 5, 2-3 a 4, 4-7 a 3, 8 or more a 2 or lower. Impact outweighs count: one error that breaks the meaning weighs more than three missing articles.

## 5. Error severity

- **Slip:** a typo, their/there, its/it's, a dropped letter, a single missing article in otherwise controlled text. Tolerated even at 5, because the rubric allows the errors a competent writer makes under timed conditions. Don't let slips move the score, but do list them so the user can cut them.
- **Noticeable:** wrong word form (economic/economical), tense or aspect (have been / had been), agreement, wrong preposition or collocation (make a trip), countability, a broken conditional. The reader notices, the meaning survives. These decide 4 vs 3.
- **Impeding:** word order or word choice that obscures the meaning; fused sentences where it is unclear what modifies what. These pull toward 2.
- **Register and conventions:** inappropriate tone, a missing greeting or closing, demands where requests belong. Judged under social conventions in the Email.

British and American spelling are both fine; mixing them is a slip at most.

## 6. Score tables

**Band and CEFR** (ETS; same for every section and the total): 6 = C2; 5.5 and 5 = C1; 4.5 and 4 = B2; 3.5 and 3 = B1; 2.5 and 2 = A2; 1.5 and 1 = A1. The overall score is the mean of the four section bands rounded to the nearest half band, with .25 rounding up (5.25 becomes 5.5, 5.125 becomes 5).

**ETS concordance, legacy Writing 0-30 to the 1-6 band** (ETS *Updated TOEFL iBT Test Overview*, Table 3):
30-29: 6 | 28-27: 5.5 | 26-24: 5 | 23-21: 4.5 | 20-17: 4 | 16-15: 3.5 | 14-13: 3 | 12-10: 2.5 | 9-5: 2 | 6-3: 1.5 | 4-0: 1
(ETS's own table overlaps at the bottom, 1.5 = 3-6, 1 = 0-4 and 2 = 5-9; it is reproduced as published. Nothing at band 3 or above is affected.)

**Legacy (pre-2026) Writing, mean task rubric score to 0-30** (ETS):

**Chained, as used by `scripts/estimate_band.py`** (mean of Email and WAD, before the BAS adjustment):
5.0: 6 | 4.5: 5.5 | 4.0: 5 | 3.5: 4.5 | 3.0: 4 | 2.5: 4 | 2.0: 3 | 1.5: 2.5 | 1.0: 2 | 0.5: 1.5 | 0: 1
Build a Sentence then lowers the estimate only when it is out of line with that level, so weaker writers aren't penalised twice. Expected minimum: 8/10 at band 5 and above, 6/10 at 4-4.5, 4/10 at 3-3.5, none below. A shortfall of 1-2 items costs 0.5 band; 3 or more costs 1.0. BAS never raises the estimate. Read the result as plus or minus 0.5 band (plus or minus 1 with a single extended task).

Why the chain is reasonable but not exact: the Academic Discussion task and rubric carried over unchanged from the pre-2026 test, where the mean rubric score mapped onto 0-30, and ETS publishes how 0-30 maps onto the new bands. But the new section adds Build a Sentence and the Email, uses a new scoring model, and ETS doesn't publish how the tasks combine; an IELTS-commissioned analysis also argues that legacy concordances may not transfer cleanly to the new test. Use the estimate to track progress, not to promise a score.

## 7. What the Writing bands mean

ETS's performance descriptors for Writing (drawn from the CEFR), summarised:

- **6 (C2):** formulates thoughts precisely with an unrestricted range; conveys fine shades of meaning with qualifying devices; idiomatic vocabulary with awareness of connotation.
- **5-5.5 (C1):** a broad range of complex structures used flexibly; layout, paragraphing and punctuation consistent and helpful; qualifies opinions by degree of certainty; disagrees diplomatically.
- **4-4.5 (B2):** well organised, coherent text with a range of linking devices; good control of simple structures plus some complex ones, used somewhat rigidly; supports points with relevant detail and examples; occasional wrong word choice that doesn't hinder communication.
- **3-3.5 (B1):** basic factual emails; a wide range of simple language; the main point comes across; major errors appear when expressing complex thoughts.
- **2-2.5 (A2):** brief everyday expressions for simple concrete needs; basic mistakes, but the meaning is usually clear.
- **1-1.5 (A1):** very short, simple messages; isolated phrases and sentences.

Use these to sanity-check the estimate. If the chained band says 5.5 but the writing never qualifies a claim or handles complex structures flexibly, say so.
