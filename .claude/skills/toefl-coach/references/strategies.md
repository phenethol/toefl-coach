# Strategies: structure, timing and score scales

This document is for the learner. Examples are in English.

## Score scales
- **Task score (0–5):** the ETS rubric score for one response (Take an Interview, Email, Academic Discussion). It does not appear on the score report.
- **Section band (1–6, in steps of 0.5):** this is what the score report shows, for each section and overall (the average of the sections).

| Band | CEFR |
|---|---|
| 6 | C2 |
| 5 | C1 |
| 4 | B2 |
| 3 | B1 |
| 2 | A2 |
| 1 | A1 |

- ETS does not publish how task scores convert into a band, so every conversion is an estimate.
- During the transition period ETS also shows a comparable score on the old 0–120 scale next to the band.

## Speaking: Take an Interview (45 s, no preparation)

### How long
- **Aim to finish at about 40–42 s**, when the timer shows 00:05–00:03.
- Don't stop before ~38 s, and don't run right up to the beep either: the real test cuts the recording at 45 s, so the conclusion gets lost.
- **For ETS, 70–90 words are enough** in short, finished sentences with a clear ending. 90–100 words is simply what fits into 40 s at a typical pace; it is not a requirement.

### A four-part structure tied to the timer
| Timer | Seconds | Part | What you say |
|---|---|---|---|
| 00:45 → 00:40 | 0–5 | **1. Direct answer** | one sentence that literally answers the question |
| 00:40 → 00:20 | 5–25 | **2. Main reason + concrete example** | the core of the answer, with one real detail (a place, a number, a situation) |
| 00:20 → 00:10 | 25–35 | **3. Second reason or concession** | *Also…* or *Of course, some people might say…, but…* |
| 00:10 → 00:03 (orange) | 35–42 | **4. Conclusion** | ***So overall…*** in one sentence that repeats the position from part 1 |

**The most important rule:** when the timer turns orange, move to the conclusion, wherever you are.

### By question type
ETS (*Test Overview*, pp. 18–19) only says that questions 1–2 are about facts and personal experience, and questions 3–4 ask for an opinion on broader issues, usually ending with *Why or why not?* Each question has a 1–2 sentence lead-in and often two sub-questions: answer **both**. The types below are our own names for the most common variants.

| Question | Type | Part 1 | Parts 2–3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | personal | *I usually… / Right now I… / The last time I…* | what happened or the situation (*for example, last week…*), then *the reason is…* |
| 2 | personal experience or reaction | *For me, it's definitely…* or *It depends on…* | reason + example, then a second reason |
| 2 | preference | *I'd definitely rather…* or *It depends on…* | reason + example, then a second reason |
| 3 | opinion | *I (mostly) agree / I don't agree with…* | reason + example, then a **concession** (*Of course…, but…*) |
| 4 | comparison | ***Both matter, but mainly…*** | why that side, then credit the other |
| 4 | cause + solution | *I think the main reason is…* | half the time on the cause, half on the solution (*As for solutions…*) |
| 4 | future | *I expect that… / It's likely that…* | why + an example from today, then a hedge (*but probably not…*) |

### Rules that always apply
1. **Answer every part of the question.** *Tell me about…* asks for a description; *would you rather / who should do more* asks for a choice.
2. **One idea, one sentence.** When you feel a third *and*, *because* or *I think* coming, put in a full stop.
3. **One well-developed idea beats three listed ones.** A concrete detail raises the score.
4. **Start within 1–2 seconds** with a ready opener: *Well, honestly…*, *I'd definitely say…*, *For me, it really depends on…*
5. **If you finish too early**, don't open a new topic. Add a detail to the example you already gave: *For instance, just yesterday…*
6. **Say *I think* once.** Alternatives: *I'd say…*, *In my view…*, *Personally…*, *I'm convinced that…*

## Writing: Academic Discussion (10 min, at least 100 words)

ETS does not prescribe a structure. The score is holistic and looks at four things: a clear position, something new that classmates haven't said, development, and precise language.

### Structure (120–160 words)
| Part | Length | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Position** | 1–2 sentences | Answer the professor's question right away. You can build on a classmate by name and announce your new angle. |
| **2. Main reason** | 1–2 sentences | One reason the classmates did **not** mention: why it is so and what follows from it. |
| **3. Concrete example** | 1–2 sentences | Your town, school, personal experience or a situation with details. This is what most often separates a 5 from a 4. |
| **4. The other side** | 1 sentence | Acknowledge the other classmate's point, then explain why your argument still stands. |
| **5. Conclusion** | 1 short sentence | Optional. Just tie it back to your position. |

### Example (topic: banning phones in secondary school; Laura: yes, they distract; Omar: no, they are useful)
> I'm mostly on Laura's side, but I think the strongest argument for a ban isn't about distraction in class; it's about what happens during breaks. When phones are allowed all day, students spend lunch and recess scrolling instead of talking to each other, so they miss out on the casual conversations that build friendships. At my secondary school, phones were collected every morning for one semester as an experiment, and the change was obvious: the cafeteria became noisy again, and students who had barely spoken to each other started playing cards together. Omar is right that phones can be useful learning tools, but that benefit doesn't require students to carry them all day; schools could simply provide tablets for the lessons that need them. In short, a ban protects something that a classroom rule alone cannot.

### Useful phrases
- **Position:** *I lean toward X's view, but…* / *I partly agree with X, although…*
- **New angle:** *What neither X nor Y mentions is…* / *An even more important factor is…*
- **Example:** *In my hometown, for instance,…* / *A good example is…*
- **The other side:** *X has a point that…, but…* / *While it's true that…, …*

### What lowers the score
- Memorized openers (*"This is a very controversial topic in today's society."*)
- Retelling the classmates' posts instead of contributing (usually 3 at most)
- A five-paragraph essay: this is a forum post
- Forcing 250+ words: more text means more errors
- Sentences of 25+ words, which are harder for both the automated scorer and the reader to follow
- An ambitious word in the wrong combination (*stalls*, *comparatively cheap to*). Precise beats fancy.

### Timing
- **1–2 min:** decide your position and your new argument
- **7 min:** write
- **1 min:** check articles (*a/the*), agreement and spelling (double letters!)

## Writing: Email (7 min, write as much as you can)
- Cover **all three points** in the task, each developed in 2–3 sentences rather than just mentioned.
- Match the **register** to the recipient (a professor or club leader: polite and formal; a friend: relaxed).
- Structure: greeting → why you are writing (1 sentence) → point 1 → point 2 → point 3 (usually a suggestion or a request) → thanks and sign-off.
